SCENE SCAFFOLD

War-Dialling the Paddocks

Rank: The Road Years Alive Tier: Medium · 2 pp Plan §8 Band: Root Alternate seat: THE happy full scene

The job — what this scene must accomplish

Two pages of the road years being actual life: a night of technical play that is also a good night in a marriage-shaped thing. Three loads, carried lightly:

  • Katita becomes the one who listens. Her whole later arc — Katita listens, Katita understands — starts here as a hobby, not a destiny. She dials, she waits, she hears. Leif watches her do it. The reversal from the rooftop (there she watched him over-tell) should be felt and never noted.
  • One machine answers. A whole night of numbers ringing into darkness, and then the handshake. The joy of it must be real — a found thing, like money in a coat — and the meaning of it (a machine saying I'm here) belongs to the reader alone.
  • Pleasure funds the fracture. The ratified ranking demands one genuinely happy road-years scene because the split must be emotionally funded. River bathing currently holds that full-scene seat; this block is its ratified alternate. Write it as a 2-page medium that could take the weight — if it swaps in, it expands to the full treatment (room, bodies, weather, silence, consequences), and the extra page goes to bodies, not to more numbers.

Where it sits

Currently remainder pp. 9–10 (≈ MS pp. 61–62) — the first block of "The Road Years Alive," straight after the origins cluster (phone box, old maps, river bathing). The order can shift on the Pressure Map; wherever it lands, it should read as a night plucked from many like it, not a milestone.

Feeds directly into: the vernacular republic (p. 11 — payphones and copper are already on that inventory), the storm (p. 15 — the mesh that answers back at scale), the single signal check of the aftermath years (one answer among dead numbers, again), and the bushranger's playlist, where the modem handshake is a canon track. Pays off from: the rooftop routing line — she is out here finding routes — and the memorised payphone number: two people who keep obsolete channels alive on purpose.

Canon — locked in the Bible

  • The event: Katita sits in the truck at night, dialling rural numbers to find live copper connections. Most ring into darkness. One answers with a modem handshake — the lonely sound of a machine saying: I'm here.canon
  • The register: the road years feel like actual life — country pubs, cheap motels, digital play at night, tools dropped in grass, poetry and mathematics passing casually between them. Not a grid for post-collapse infrastructure.canon
  • ROOT arises from pleasure, play and curiosity, not fear of collapse. This night is pleasure.canon
  • The technology: handmade, local, weathered, tender — never sleek sci-fi. Low-power ROOT downloads at ~300 bits/sec; the truck is the third character.canon

Dialogue permissions

Katita may

Keep a tally with her own marks — ring-out, disconnected, changed. Read exchange prefixes like stations on a line. Count rings aloud, quietly, out of habit rather than method. Laugh at a recorded disconnection message. Hand Leif the handset at exactly the right moment. Log the number that answered without ceremony and not dial it again.

Leif may

Be the audience for once. Ask non-technical questions ("how far away is that one?"). Drowse and be woken. Memorise the number that answered without deciding to — the same reflex as the payphone. Make tea on the tailgate stove and get the order of operations wrong.

No one may

Say lonely, alive, listening, waiting, archive. Explain what war-dialling is, or what the handshake means, or why she does it. Treat the answering machine as an omen, a lead, or a plan — nobody proposes driving there. The night must not know it is being kept.

Sensory bank — raw material, take what serves

  • A gravel pull-off between paddocks; cab light off so the moths go for the laptop screen instead; the truck ticking as it cools.
  • A shire phone book, water-swollen, bought for fifty cents at a CWA book sale — some of the numbers in it are for the dead. She dials them anyway; a number outlives its house.
  • The dial tone's flat hum; pulse-clicks down the line; the long-distance hiss like wind in the wire — which, out here, is nearly what it is.
  • Ring cadence going somewhere real: a hall table, a crocheted doily, a hallway dark since the clearing sale. Ten rings is her limit. Twelve for numbers she likes the shape of.
  • The green Telstra pillar box at the road edge, lichen on its lid — copper pairs fanning out under the paddocks like a root system nobody digs up.
  • Disconnection recordings in that bright dead voice; a changed-number announcement for a stock agent three owners gone.
  • The handshake, when it comes: carrier tone, then the answering scream and crash of negotiation — two machines finding a speed they can both bear — settling into a held chord.
  • Outside: frost forming on the bonnet; a windmill clanking once as the air shifts; a dog barking two properties over, then giving it up; the Milky Way with the density it only has past the last streetlight.
  • Inside: thermos tea in the shared lid; her bare feet on the dash; the biro from his beard on the tally sheet; the inverter's whine under everything like a held breath.
  • The truck as the only lit room for forty kilometres — one warm cab in a paddock of dark houses, ringing them one by one.

Traps — the known failure modes

  • Sentimentalising the handshake. "The lonely sound of a machine saying I'm here" is the writer's gloss, not the page's. If the prose says lonely, the moment is spent. Render the sound physically and let the reader do the aching.
  • The infodump. No paragraph explaining war-dialling, exchanges, or copper. The reader learns the practice the way Leif did: by watching her do it.
  • Plot leak. The answering machine is not a mystery to solve, a node to visit, or a person to find. If the scene generates a to-do item, cut the to-do item.
  • Elegy drift. Dead numbers, dark farmhouses, frost — the ingredients want to be mournful. This is a happy scene. The dominant sensation is the pleasure of two people who like their work and each other. Melancholy gets one grace note at most.
  • The swap trap. If this trades seats with river bathing and becomes the full scene, the extra page must go to room, bodies, weather, silence, consequences — not to more numbers, more exchanges, more tally. The list is already long enough at two pages.

Ten sparks — optional ways in, steal freely

  1. Open on the tally sheet, not the phone: her private notation — a dash for ring-out, a hook for disconnected, a dot for anything strange — legible to no one, including him.
  2. The phone book's front pages: tide times for a coast three hundred kilometres away, poison schedules, a 1987 map of the shire. She dials in page order, like reading.
  3. She narrates the prefixes as geography — this exchange is the one past the silos, this one is the pub with the good dog — building the district by ear before they've driven half of it.
  4. One number is answered by a person: an old woman, late, not angry, just awake. Katita apologises in the voice you use in someone's hallway and hangs up. Two minutes of quiet after. The near-miss human is what makes the machine's answer land.
  5. Leif is asleep when it answers. She holds the handset a long moment, alone with it, then decides — wake him or keep it. Whichever she does plants who she becomes: the one at ease being the only listener.
  6. The handshake heard with one earpiece between two heads, temple to temple — the same geometry as the napkin at the party, nobody remembering that.
  7. She writes the answering number on his forearm with the beard biro because the tally sheet has gone under the seat. He doesn't wash it off. One touch; no comment.
  8. The count: two hundred and eleven numbers over three nights, or however many the prose can carry — the scale delivered once, dryly, as her arithmetic, not the narrator's wonder.
  9. After the handshake they go outside into the frost to stand in the dark they've been ringing. The paddock is exactly as silent as it was. That's the joke, and neither says it.
  10. End on the discipline: she logs the number, caps the pen, doesn't redial. "Enough routes," is all she says — to the tally sheet, not to him — and the reader may or may not catch the echo.

Exit condition

Two pages, ending the same night, nothing decided and nothing planned. The reader should close the scene warmed — a good night among many — and only later feel what was in it: the woman who listens being formed, the one answer in the dark that the whole aftermath will rhyme with. If the scene swaps into the full-scene seat, it earns its third page with skin and weather, and its exit stays identical: no consequence visible to the people inside it. If it feels like the beginning of something while it's happening, it isn't finished.
