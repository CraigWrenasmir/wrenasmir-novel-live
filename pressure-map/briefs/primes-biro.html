SCENE SCAFFOLD

Primes on Skin / The Biro

Rank: Origins & Pleasure Tier: Fleck · 1 p Band: Root Plan §6: bodily languages Plants the final added beat

The job — what this scene must accomplish

One page, one dark room, one protocol. This fleck installs the couple's first private language and hides a fuse that burns for the rest of the book. Three loads, all carried in the dark:

  • The tapping game exists. Katita taps sequences on Leif's forearm; he guesses prime or not prime; he is often wrong and the wrongness is part of the pleasure. Touch, repetition, willing failure — a game, not a cipher.
  • The biro mark is made and kept. She writes something small on his skin. He does not wash it off. The page must not announce the keeping — it just happens, the way a man keeps a receipt.
  • The body becomes a temporary page — but silently. The rhyme family (Andrew's mermaid tattoo, the courtesan's bathwater text, bark and file) already exists in the manuscript; this page joins the family without a single member being named.

Fuse ruling (Craig, 2026-07-22 — inverts the original design): this page MAY close the circuit openly — the drafted scene ends with Leif tapping fifty-seven into the train window "all these years on," returning the reader to the carriage. Declared plant, subtle echo: because the wire is connected here, the later Tapping Returns fleck (p. 40) works by restraint — an echo passing almost unremarked — and the arrival still resolves it by one added beat as pure rhythm. Fleck discipline otherwise unchanged: flash and vanish; the miniature is the strength.

Where it sits

Currently remainder p. 3 (≈ MS p. 55) — directly after the rooftop party. It can be the same night or soon after; the page should not say which. The order can shift on the Pressure Map, but the adjacency to the rooftop is doing work: the pen pulled from his beard at the party is the natural ancestor of the biro now writing on him.

Feeds directly into: Play Before Mission (pp. 4–5 — the same appetite for shared, useless, repeatable pleasure moves onto screens) and, far downstream, the unconscious tapping of the aftermath years. Pays off from: the rooftop — the pen, the theft, the first intimacy already being a writing implement.

Canon — locked in the Bible

  • The game: Katita taps sequences against Leif's forearm in the dark. He guesses prime or not prime, often wrongly. The pleasure lies in touch, repetition and willing failure.canon
  • The biro annotation: Katita writes a word, correction, coordinate, number or tiny image on Leif's skin. He does not wash it off. The reader realises later that he is still carrying it.canon
  • The rhyme family (never named on the page): Andrew's faded mermaid tattoo; the courtesan's bath-water text (the title in dissolved ink on her arm, MS pp. 25–26); clothes retaining bodily impressions; the archive writing through bark, files and memory. The body becomes a temporary page.canon
  • The long payoff: ten years on, Leif taps a rhythm unconsciously — motel room, motorbike, train window. It is simultaneously her game, the page-one ostinato, bodily memory, a protocol, a phrase awaiting resolution. Near the end the slowing train may resolve it by one added beat.canon
  • The relationship begins in play — the whole Origins band answers to this. No mission exists yet; ROOT is not even a joke yet.canon
  • States: both of them inside pleasure, neither of them inside meaning. Katita is the author of the protocol; Leif is its glad, fallible receiver. No one is teaching anyone anything.canon

Dialogue permissions

Katita may

Explain the rules once, badly, half-asleep. Answer a wrong guess with another sequence instead of words. Laugh in the dark where he can only feel it through the mattress. Write on him without asking — the same licence as the pen-theft at the party. Refuse to say what she wrote, or lie about it cheerfully.

Leif may

Guess with fake confidence and be wrong. Lose count when a car goes past and ask for the number again — and be given a different number instead. Ask what the mark says. Hold his arm up to the streetlight stripe to read it. Say nine is prime and defend it briefly.

No one may

Say code, protocol, message, memory, skin as page or anything in that family. Explain why willing failure is sweet. Notice that the game is one-way and remark on it. Know that the tapping will outlive the relationship. And the biro mark must never be a loaded word — if it's root, shore, organ, a motif or a thesis, cut it: a phone-number fragment, a correction to something he misquoted, a small fish, a coordinate. Ordinary cargo only.

Sensory bank — raw material, take what serves

  • A box fan on the floorboards turning its head away and back; the sheet already kicked to the foot of the bed; streetlight through venetians laying stripes across both of them.
  • Fingernail versus fingertip: two different consonants on the skin. Eleven taps take long enough that a whole car passes through the middle of them.
  • Two is almost nothing — a tap-tap so small he thinks she's fallen asleep inside the number.
  • The biro: the cold ballpoint dot of first contact; ink skipping over arm hair; skin pulled taut with her other hand; the pen dragged slower than writing on paper, because skin moves.
  • Smells: ballpoint ink, hot night, mosquito coil dead in its saucer, cask wine still in the room from earlier.
  • The shower after (that night or next morning): soaping around the forearm; the ink greying at the edges but holding; a man washing around something without ceremony.
  • The arithmetic as physical sensation: odd numbers feel pointier; nine feels prime and isn't; he does the dividing with his lips moving slightly and she can feel his jaw against her hair.
  • Somewhere down the line a freight train shunts once — one free ostinato echo, don't underline it.
  • Sweat making the fresh ink bloom a fraction wider by morning, like a word relaxing.

Traps — the known failure modes

  • The cipher. If the tapping reads as cryptography foreshadowing — if the prose gets interested in primes as mathematics — the fleck dies. It's foreplay. The numbers matter the way the rules of a drinking game matter.
  • Explaining the pleasure. Do not narrate why being wrong is sweet. Show him guessing wrong and settling deeper into the pillow. The reader does the rest.
  • The loaded word. The single biggest risk on the page is what the biro says. Anything thematic turns the mark into a label. Keep the cargo ordinary and slightly withheld.
  • Announcing the keeping. "He never washed it off" is a fact the book proves later by the mark still being there. If this page states it as a vow, the payoff is spent at the wrong end.
  • Chronology certification. The page must not say this was early, this was the beginning, they would play it for years. One night. Backstage stays backstage.
  • Fleck bloat. One page. If it wants a second, it's stealing from River Bathing.

Ten sparks — optional ways in, steal freely

  1. She teaches the rules wrong-way-first: taps four, he says prime, and she keeps his wrong answer as house rule for the rest of the night — the game's first act is accepting an error as law, and neither of them comments.
  2. He can identify her mood by tap tempo before he can identify a single prime — impatient sevens, drowsy elevens. He never gets better at the maths; he gets better at her.
  3. Open on the count, not the couple: a bare number of taps arriving out of darkness, the reader doing the arithmetic with him, wrong.
  4. The biro is the party pen — she never gave it back after the napkin. It lives behind her ear now. Writing on him is how it finally returns to its owner.
  5. What she writes is a correction: he misquoted something earlier that evening and she prints the right word on the inside of his forearm, where he'll read it every time he reaches for anything.
  6. Nine ruins him every time, so she starts using nine as punctuation — an endearment, the number that means him. Never glossed.
  7. He tries to tap back once and she flattens her palm over his knuckles until he stops. The game goes one way. That's the game.
  8. Headlights sweep the ceiling mid-sequence, he loses count and asks her to start again; she starts a different number instead. No number is ever repeated. He learns not to ask.
  9. The shower: he soaps everything and holds one forearm out of the spray like a man carrying something across a river. Four lines, no interiority.
  10. End on orientation, not meaning: the mark reads upside-down to anyone else in the world and right-way-up only when he turns his arm the way you turn it to check your own watch.

Exit condition

One page. It ends in the dark or in the shower, on an image of keeping rather than a thought about keeping — no vow, no forward glance. The reader should file it as erotic miscellany, a small good thing between two people, and forget it the way you forget a rhythm you've absorbed. Amended per the fuse ruling (2026-07-22): the page may end on the train-window hinge — the reader is allowed to know the rhythm survives. What is still forbidden: naming what the rhythm means, glossing the keeping as a vow, or letting the narration promise a resolution. The reader may know it comes back; they may not know it resolves. The added beat still belongs to the arrival.
