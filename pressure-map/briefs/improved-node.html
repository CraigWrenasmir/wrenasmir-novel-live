SCENE SCAFFOLD

The Improved Node as Rumour

Rank: Ten Years of Aftermath Tier: Fleck · 1 p Plan §17 Band: root Law: RUMOUR > PROOF

The job — what this scene must accomplish

A fleck — flash and vanish. The strongest evidence of continuation in the whole book arrives secondhand, degraded in transmission, told by someone who cares about something else. Three loads:

  • Deliver the rumour, never the object. A module in a fig tree in another state — better solar, unfamiliar casing, recognisable logic. Leif hears it; he does not see it; the reader never will.
  • Hold five possibilities open at once, ranked by no one: Katita built it · someone copied their work · the project became folk technology · ROOT altered its own design · civilisation continuing through anonymous uptake and revision.
  • Protect the law of the block: this rumour is stronger than definitive proof that Katita is still on the road would be. The scene must be built so the reader feels the superiority of the unresolved version without being told why hearsay outweighs an instrument.

The signal check (pp. 36–37) gave him a reading and it settled nothing. A stranger's mangled story gives him more than the reading did. That asymmetry is the fleck's whole argument — made by placement, never by commentary.

Where it sits

Currently remainder p. 38 (≈ MS p. 90) — second block of the Aftermath rank, directly after the signal check. The order can shift, but the strong sequence is instrument-first, rumour-second: the device answers in timestamps, then a pub anecdote answers in kind. It should not open the rank.

Feeds directly into: Imagined Node Conversations (p. 39) — the rumour hands his imagination its raw material. Pays off from: the napkin topology (the recognisable logic is hers and his, decades on, in a stranger's mouth) and the vernacular republic (public memory travelling by anecdote, misquoted and alive).

Dialogue permissions

The teller may

Get every word wrong while keeping the structure right — "solar whatsit," "weather station," "bat box with delusions." Care more about the fig, the council, or their own part in the story than about the thing itself. Contradict themselves on details that don't matter and hold firm on the one that does: the workmanship.

Leif may

Recognise the logic through the mangling — from the detail the teller found pointless. Ask one question too many, hear himself, and stop. Buy the next round to keep the teller on the fig and off the module. Say "huh." Not ask which state.

No one may

Use ROOT vocabulary correctly. Say or think Katita's name within a page of this block. Rank the five possibilities — no "probably," no "surely not," no adverbs of likelihood. Propose going to see it, note the town's name, or keep the story for later use. Understand, on the page, what has been said.

Sensory bank — raw material, take what serves

  • The fig, offstage but vivid in the telling: a Moreton Bay fig at a showground or school edge in another state — buttress roots like walls, a canopy that owns its own weather, flying foxes at dusk.
  • The teller: a powerline-clearance contractor, tree lopper or travelling fencer at the bar — someone paid to be up trees, with sawdust in the sock line and a dog tied outside.
  • Pub texture: meat-raffle tickets in a beer jug, a fan turning slower than the flies, the till drawer's bell, Sunday session light through tinted glass.
  • The story's surviving details, weathered by retelling: a panel "too good for the bush," a casing colour nothing comes in, screws where screws shouldn't be, the whole thing "wired to nothing but looking like it was expecting company."
  • Provenance already degraded: the contractor got it from a brother-in-law, or from another crew, or saw it himself two seasons back — the story unsure of its own custody.
  • The beer sweating between them; the teller's hands drawing the fig's size in the air — generous, then more generous.
  • What the teller did with it: nothing. Left it where it was. "Wasn't hurting anyone." Respect for workmanship as bush ethics.
  • Afterwards: the ride out of town, paddocks, a windbreak on a far rise looked at slightly too long.

Traps — the known failure modes

  • Scene-jumping to the fig. If the module appears in scene-time — even one rendered image of it in place — the rumour is dead and the law is broken. It exists only inside the teller's syntax.
  • The verification itch. Leif noting the town, planning a detour, asking which state: any of these turns a fleck into a plot. The not-asking is the scene's spine.
  • Ranking the five. The possibilities may pass through as images, but the moment one is likelier than another, the superposition collapses. No adjudication, no order of appeal.
  • The knowing messenger. If the teller understands what they found, rumour becomes report. Their attention must keep sliding off the module toward the fig, the council, the brother-in-law.
  • Proof-shaped detail. No serial numbers, no initials in the casing, no handwriting, nothing dateable. Any detail a reader could file as evidence must be blunted in retelling.
  • Underlining the law. "Somehow this meant more than proof would have" — the exact sentence the scene must never contain. The asymmetry is built, not stated.

Ten sparks — optional ways in, steal freely

  1. The story arrives as a complaint: the council wanted the fig lopped, the contractor found "some hobbyist's gear" in the crown, and the grievance is about the paperwork, not the machine.
  2. Wrong words carrying right structure — the teller's best sentence is an accident: "Wired to nothing, but set up like it was expecting company."
  3. The detail that hooks Leif is the solar: "panel on it better than the one on me caravan." The improvement lands as a tradesman's envy, which is the most credible witness there is.
  4. Leif recognises the design from the part the teller found pointless — why would you want one, out there, talking to nothing? The pointless bit is the routing. He says nothing.
  5. Provenance squared: the contractor half-admits he got the story from his brother-in-law's crew. Two removes. The rumour arrives already revised — like everything else that survives.
  6. Leif shouts the round to keep the teller talking about the fig — steering away from the module, because asking after it directly would spend it.
  7. The five possibilities pass through him as images, half a sentence each, unranked: her hands at a bench somewhere; a stranger's copy; a shire full of them; a design revising itself like handwriting loosening. Then the raffle is drawn.
  8. He doesn't ask which state. The teller says "up north" or "over the border" and the sentence is allowed to end there, forever.
  9. That night, the comfort is the improvement itself — that whatever made it had gotten better at it. The sentence stops before it can audition builders.
  10. End on the fig, not the node: buttress roots, flying foxes, a tree large enough to hide anything it chooses, in a state that goes unnamed for the rest of the book.

Exit condition

One page, most of it the teller's voice and pub texture; the module itself gets a handful of sentences and no adjectives it didn't earn in transmission. End with the story finished and nothing done about it — no note, no plan, no name. The reader should feel this fleck land harder than the signal check that preceded it, and should not be able to say why the hearsay outweighed the instrument. If the scene explains the asymmetry, or if anyone could mark one of the five possibilities as favourite, it has failed.
