SCENE SCAFFOLD

The Vernacular Republic

Rank: The Road Years Alive Tier: Fleck · 1 p Plan §9 Band: Form The implication is never spoken

Standing notice

RE-SCOPED 2026-07-26 — FILE TREE ONLY. The organs were read by eye on the fused road page (bakery boards, honour shields, guest books, radio logs); this page renders the same organs as a ROOT catalogue — the battery installed, made visible as form. The prose-list version is spent; this must be machine-shaped or not exist.

The job — what this scene must accomplish

One page, and it is a formal page — a shape in the book's shifting-text tradition, not a scene. Its work:

  • Show the country already doing it. Australia's existing public memory-organs laid out as an inventory: the reader sees a republic of chalkboards and honour boards that has been transmitting all along. ROOT's true ancestry, presented without a word of ancestry.
  • Carry the implication silently. ROOT does not invent public memory; it gives the vernacular republic a battery. That sentence is backstage law. It must arise from the arrangement of the list — from what sits next to what, from a column heading, from an omission — never from prose, never from a character's mouth.
  • Behave like a pressure peak. In this book, form arrives where language can't quite hold itself. The inventory should feel found or overheard — a register the road produced — not composed. Advise the eye, don't perform for it.

Where it sits

Currently remainder p. 11 (≈ MS p. 63) — directly after war-dialling and before the Big Thing. Good placement: the night of live copper flows straight into the daylight census of everything else that carries the district's memory. The order can shift on the Pressure Map; this page is portable, and would also survive sitting after the storm as a cooling page.

Feeds directly into: everything ROOT does from here — every module is now legible (to the reader only) as a battery clipped onto something that already existed. Pays off from: taped books (the historian's shed), microfiche (the masthead hunt), payphones (the memorised number), country radio (the Boxer's pub backrooms). The list is half made of things the book has already touched — that's its authority.

Dialogue permissions

The page may

Take the shape of a found register: motel-book ruling, a pamphlet's contents page, a field audit in a hand that could be Katita's. Misfile one entry. Abbreviate like a person in a hurry. Break off mid-line and resume. Include a custodian column, a range column, an "updated" column — the battery implication is allowed to live in a column heading, never in a sentence.

The prose around it may

Enter the list mid-sentence — a paragraph about a counter lunch fracturing into inventory the way this book's language spills into form under pressure — and pick itself up afterwards on a small physical detail, as if nothing happened.

No one may

Caption the list. Say memory, archive, organ, republic, battery, network anywhere near it. Have Leif or Katita observe that the country already does what ROOT does. Compare any item to a node. The moment the page explains itself, it becomes an essay wearing a costume.

Sensory bank — raw material, take what serves

One concrete instance per organ, so the list can be texture rather than taxonomy:

  • Chalkboard: pub specials half-erased under tonight's; "meat tray R. Cowan" from a raffle three weeks won.
  • Honour board: gold leaf, the same surname four times, one name with a small asterisk nobody living can gloss.
  • Pub PA: the barmaid calling a phone call for a man by his nickname; the whole room knowing who without turning.
  • Racing guide read aloud: Saturday arvo, one man reading the fields to another whose eyes are gone, both disagreeing with the weights.
  • CB handles: channel 40; a man known for three hundred kilometres as a name his mother never used.
  • Motel register: biro ghosts pressed through from the page above; whole decades of "purpose of visit: passing through."
  • Handwritten menu: laminated, prices taped over twice, the tape yellower than the laminate.
  • Newsletter: church or CWA, duplicator-smelling, staples rusted into the fold; births, deaths, working bees.
  • Payphone: directory on a chain, coin slot polished by thumbs, the booth light the only civic lighting after ten.
  • Local-history pamphlet: staple-bound, typed one side, sold from an honesty box at the museum that opens Sundays 1–4.
  • Railway announcement: half-eaten by the PA, the town's name arriving intact and nothing else.
  • Taped books: the library western bound in figure-eights of masking tape so it can endure more reading. One touch; it already rhymes; no comment.
  • Microfiche: reels in shoeboxes behind the library counter; the reader's hot lamp; scanner bloom eating a word.
  • Country radio: funeral notices read on air between the stock report and the weather; the announcer pronouncing every name correctly because he has to face them.

Traps — the known failure modes

  • The caption. One sentence of framing ("everywhere they went, the country was already remembering") converts the page from evidence to thesis. The list must be trusted naked.
  • The wink. A file tree with a directory called /root/ or /battery/, an entry annotated "cf. modules" — any joke that shows the page knows what it's for. This is the no-motif-knows-it's-a-motif warning at its most acute.
  • Nostalgia. These organs are not dying quaintness; they are working infrastructure. Write them in present tense, in use, with users. Elegy turns the republic into a museum and kills the point.
  • Completeness. Fourteen organs is already generous for one page. Resist adding categories; resist a taxonomy so tidy it resolves what should stay vernacular. Let one entry be misfiled or truncated — the register should err the way its keepers do.
  • Orphaning the form. A formal page dropped between scenes with no pressure behind it reads as a gimmick. It needs to spill out of prose that was already straining — arrive mid-sentence or out of an accumulating passage of road detail, per the book's own rules.

Ten sparks — optional ways in, steal freely

  1. The motel register treatment: ruled columns — NAME / OF / PURPOSE — with the fourteen organs entered as guests, each with a date and a town. The republic checked in one per line.
  2. The field audit: Katita's hand, three columns — organ, range, custodian. The custodian column does the silent work: publican · the secretary · whoever's on the platform · anyone with a coin.
  3. The file tree: plain directory listing, no clever names, last-modified dates spanning ninety years — an honour board modified 1953, a chalkboard modified this morning.
  4. Mid-sentence fracture: a paragraph about waiting for a counter lunch loses its grip on syntax and the inventory pours through the gap; prose resumes at "and the schnitzel came out."
  5. The contents page: the whole inventory disguised as the contents of a local-history pamphlet — as if some shire historian had already written the book ROOT thinks it's inventing. (Dangerous and rich; keep the pamphlet fictional and unremarked.)
  6. The diagnostic list: status flags only a maintenance mind would write — OK · INTERMITTENT · CUSTODIAN DECEASED, RECORD CONTINUES. That third flag is the page's whole cargo; use it once.
  7. One day's drive: the organs listed in the order encountered between two towns, with odometer readings — the inventory as a strip of road, not a system.
  8. The update column: each organ with its cadence — hourly · Saturdays · yearly · when someone dies. Frequency as the quiet common denominator; no unit ever named.
  9. The repaired page: one line of the list broken and resumed with a visible seam, the way the taped western is broken and resumed — form quoting the bindery without footnoting it.
  10. The omission: the list stops at fourteen and leaves one ruled line empty. What belongs on the empty line is never supplied — by the page, by the book, by anyone.

Exit condition

One page. The reader turns it having seen a country that was already transmitting — and should not be able to quote a single sentence that told them so, because there isn't one. If a first reader can say what the page "means," it has failed; if they find themselves remembering the honour board or the funeral notices two hundred pages later at the final station, it has worked. Flash and vanish.
