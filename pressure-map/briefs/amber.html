SCENE SCAFFOLD

Amber on the Dashboard

Rank: Co-Authorship Emerges Tier: Fleck · 1 p Endgame Movement 9 Object survives the scene Condition: TRANSFORMATION

The job — what this scene must accomplish

One page: lightning splits a node-tree, and the salvage is a single object — cooked resin hardened over the board, data and tree wound sealed together. The fleck must do three things and vanish:

  • Stage the fusion as fact, not figure. The circuitry and the tree are now one material. Nobody may say what that means; the object simply exists and is heavy and smells of burnt sugar.
  • Get the piece onto the dashboard. Katita keeps it — casually, the way people keep a good stone. The keeping is her whole philosophy performed in one pocketing gesture, years before she'd own the philosophy.
  • Make the object durable in the reader's memory. It survives the scene and must be plantable in later dashboard glimpses at one touch each — because after the split it becomes an absence-marker. A page of prose has to buy an object the book can grieve with.

Where it sits

Currently remainder p. 25 (≈ MS p. 77) — after Organic Integration, before Small Private Violations, though the order can shift. The sequencing logic: the resin has been creeping over the screws for a page already; the lightning finishes in one night what the tree was doing by the season. Consummation, not accident.

Feeds directly into: the dashboard's later one-touch appearances across the road and fracture ranks, and — after the split — the absence-marker payoff. (Note: Katita keeps the truck, so where the amber ends up when they part is the split scene's decision — see Plants.) Pays off from: Organic Integration (slow resin, now flash-set) and the storm crossing the mesh — the weather that once moved through the network as sensation now writes on it as wound.

Dialogue permissions

Katita may

Assess the strike practically — what's recoverable, what isn't. Pick the piece up bare-handed before it's quite cool. Answer "what do you want that for" with nothing, or with something under four words. Put it on the dash and adjust it once, so it sits.

Leif may

Grieve the node in inventory terms — board, cells, three months of recordings. Photograph the split trunk for the log. Suggest, without heat, that the piece is salvage and belongs in the parts box. Lose that exchange without it becoming an argument.

No one may

Say what the object is an image of. Use amber's museum register — preservation, millennia, insects in golden light — aloud. Mourn ceremonially. Connect the strike to any of the rank's other strangeness. It was a storm; storms hit trees; they drive on.

Sensory bank — raw material, take what serves

  • The morning after: ozone gone, wet ash smell staying; the split trunk white and wrenched, heartwood shining like something private shown suddenly in daylight.
  • Burnt sugar and eucalyptus — cooked sap; kino bled and boiled black at the edges, honey-clear at the centre.
  • The board inside the set resin like a leaf in a paperweight: tracks visible, a component's number legible through a centimetre of gold, solder run into little rivulets and frozen.
  • The piece's physical facts: heavier than it looks, faintly tacky on one face for weeks, warm out of the wreck and holding warmth longer than seems right.
  • Salvage choreography: gloves not bothered with, the crowbar's chirp against charred bark, pieces sorted onto a tarp into works, parts, neither — the amber belonging to neither and getting kept anyway.
  • The dashboard as habitat: dust, a dead biro, service dockets folded to coasters, the piece catching low sun on west roads and putting one amber coin of light on the cab ceiling.
  • On corners it slides its few centimetres and knocks the windscreen — a small knuckle-tap the cab learns and stops hearing.
  • Heat: left in the windscreen sun the tacky face softens slightly and the piece sticks — over months it settles into one spot and makes itself a ring.

Traps — the known failure modes

  • The symbol speech. If anyone — character or narration — glosses the amber (memory preserved, wound and data one flesh), the object dies on the page. The fleck's power is that everyone treats it as a knick-knack.
  • Museum amber. The word carries fossils and gift shops. Keep the material in the tradesman's register — sap, kino, set resin — and let amber land once, plainly, as the colour it is.
  • Ceremony. No burial for the node, no minute's silence. Salvage is brisk. The tenderness is in the sorting tarp, not in observance.
  • Foreshadowing the split. The absence-marker payoff belongs downstream. If the keeping is written wistfully — if Katita's pocketing gesture is lit with future loss — the plant is showing. It should read as a woman who likes a good object.
  • Overweight. A fleck that runs past its page has stolen from a full scene. One strike, one salvage, one object placed. Vanish.

Ten sparks — optional ways in, steal freely

  1. Open on the smell before the sight — burnt sugar on the track in, wrong for the hour, so the body knows before the inventory does.
  2. The strike reported first by the network: neighbouring nodes logged the storm's approach the way they logged the crossing years ago, and one entry simply stops mid-column. The blank in the log arrives before the wrecked tree does.
  3. Salvage as triage: the tarp's three piles, and the piece that fails all three categories — neither working, nor part, nor rubbish — becoming, by elimination, a possession.
  4. Katita holds the piece up to the sun and reads a component number aloud through the resin, once, like reading a fortune she has no intention of believing. Then pockets it.
  5. Leif's version of keeping: he photographs the split trunk for the log, twice, from the angles the insurance would want if there were insurance. Two archives of the same wound made in the same minute — nobody compares them.
  6. The trunk itself: split but not felled, one green side still working, the node's mounting bracket now holding nothing at head height like a sconce. They leave the bracket. Unscrewing it would be the sentimental act.
  7. The piece's first drive: it slides on the first three corners, and Katita, without looking, puts one finger on it each time until it learns its spot. That finger is the scene's whole emotional budget.
  8. A week of little uses: paperweight for dockets, sun-catcher, something to turn over during a long fuel stop. The object earns its dashboard tenure through utility, not sentiment.
  9. Heat variant: days later, forgotten in windscreen sun, it softens enough to hold a thumbprint. One of them notices the print. The page doesn't say whose thumb, or who noticed.
  10. End on the tap: night driving, corrugated road, the small knuckle-knock of resin on glass at each cattle grid — the cab's new sound, unremarked, already part of the truck's vocabulary as the page cuts out.

Exit condition

One page, and the object has changed jurisdiction: from tree to dashboard, from wreckage to furniture. Nothing has been said about it that couldn't be said about a good stone. The reader should register the amber as a charming detail and only much later — when the dashboard is glimpsed without comment, and later still when the split empties the cab — understand what the page was doing. If the amber glows with meaning now, it will have nothing left to do then.
