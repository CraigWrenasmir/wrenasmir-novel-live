SCENE SCAFFOLD

The Invented Big Thing

Rank: The Road Years Alive Tier: Fleck · 1 p Plan §8 Band: Root More than comic

The job — what this scene must accomplish

One page, one object. An invented Australian roadside monument that lands first as roadside comedy and stays as something heavier. Three loads:

  • Invent it properly. Not the Banana, not the Merino — a fictional Big Thing with the true grammar of the genre (fibreglass or plate steel, a plinth, a car park, a plaque) and one wrongness: its original purpose has become obscure. Nobody, including the town, can quite say what it was for.
  • Carry the metaphysical charge lightly. This is what remains of a town that once tried to make itself legible from the highway. The dread and tenderness of that must come off the object itself — rust, scale, birds — never off a sentence about towns or legibility.
  • Stay a fleck. Flash and vanish. They pull over, they stand in its shadow, they drive on. The page may be revisited later (Leif alone, in the aftermath decade) but this block spends its single page in the road years, together.

Where it sits

Currently remainder p. 12 (≈ MS p. 64) — after the vernacular republic inventory, before the heritage tour of the dead server. Strong hinge position: the failed civic broadcast in steel on one page, the abandoned civic render in polygons on the next — the reader's eye makes the rhyme, the book never does. The order can shift on the Pressure Map; the fleck is portable anywhere in the road band.

Feeds directly into: the heritage tour (obscured civic purpose, again), the wren nest in the dead module (a big made thing repurposed by birds, again), Leif's aftermath decade of returning to sites alone. Pays off from: the old maps fleck — if the Big Thing is on the old map and off the new one, or vice versa, one of the maps is already wrong in the glove box.

Canon — locked in the Bible

  • The object: a strange Australian roadside monument — an abandoned civic object whose original purpose has become obscure. Rusting, enormous, perhaps bird-filled.canon
  • The charge: the metaphysical weight of a town that once tried to make itself legible from the highway.canon
  • The register: more than comic — it belongs to the road years as lived pleasure (landscapes enjoyed for their own sake, roadside humour), so the comedy is real; it just isn't the floor.canon
  • Tier: fleck — flash and vanish, per the ratified scene-time ranking.canon
  • The four warnings apply: no character states what the monument means; the object must not know it's a motif.canon

Staging latitude (plan-level, not locked): they encounter it together during the road years; Leif may later return to it alone. If both, the return belongs to the aftermath pages, not here.

Dialogue permissions

Katita may

Photograph it the way she photographed the working phone box — one frame, no ceremony. Pace out its footprint. Read the plaque aloud and stop where the plaque stops. Ask at the servo what it is and receive the answer with complete satisfaction, whatever it is.

Leif may

Love it immediately and without analysis. Know, or half-know, one fact about it that contradicts the servo's version. Touch the steel where the paint has gone and feel the day's heat still in it. Want to stay ten minutes longer than the stop needed.

No one may

Compare it to the nodes, the archive, or anything they are building. Call it sad, or a monument to anything. Use the words legible, civic, memory, forgotten. Solve it — the answers they get must not converge, and neither Leif, Katita nor the narrator may adjudicate between them.

Sensory bank — raw material, take what serves

  • Visible for twenty minutes before arrival — a shape on the heat shimmer that keeps refusing to resolve into anything, then does, and is worse.
  • Scale up close: bolt heads the size of fists; seams of rivets like sutures; a ladder inside welded off at head height, decades ago, after some incident nobody names.
  • Rust streaking down from every fixing like tidelines; municipal paint in exactly two coats — mission brown over council green — flaking to show both histories at once.
  • Birds inside: starlings or galahs, audible before visible, the whole object ticking and rustling at dusk like something coming to a low idle. A white spatter-line below every opening.
  • Wind across an aperture producing one low note, sometimes, in a crosswind. One touch, no comment, and do not let it happen at a meaningful moment.
  • The car park: room for forty cars, holding one. A broken tap. A picnic table with its centre plank gone. A bin chained to a post, the bin long stolen, the chain faithful.
  • The plaque: bronze, raised letters polished blank across the middle by forty years of thumbs — the dates legible at the edges, the purpose worn away exactly where hands could reach.
  • The town beyond: off the bypass now, its welcome sign still doing arithmetic about how far back the turn was.
  • The servo answers (keep them incompatible): it was for the scheme; it was for the centenary; it was half of something bigger that never came from the foundry; a bloke built it and the council took it over; it was always like that.
  • Smells: hot steel, dry grass gone to seed through the concrete apron, sunscreen, the pie bought at the last town cooling on the dash.

Traps — the known failure modes

  • Comedy only. If the page is a bit — quirky Australia, big dumb object — the canon charge is lost. The comedy is the way in; the object must outstay the joke by one beat.
  • Allegory. The Big Thing must never stand for ROOT, the archive, or the novel. It is a thing by a road. The moment the prose angles it toward theme, cut the angle.
  • The wrong invention. No Big Antenna, Big Ear, Big Book, Big Tree — anything that rhymes with the book's machinery is a motif that knows itself. Pick something whose obscurity is native to it: industrial, agricultural, commemorative, disputed.
  • Solving it. Three answers at the servo that triangulate to a truth are a puzzle, not a mystery. The accounts must be flatly incompatible and equally confident, and stay that way.
  • Overstaying. One page. If the scene grows a conversation about towns, the highway, or what gets remembered, it has become a medium scene about theme — kill it back to the object, the birds, the drive away.

Ten sparks — optional ways in, steal freely

  1. The Wheel: a riveted steel wheel ten metres across, upright on a plinth — turbine? mill gear? memorial? The plaque says RESTORED 1968, which means it was already old and already obscure by then.
  2. The Drop: a colossal sheet-metal water droplet, built for the opening of an irrigation scheme that was cancelled between fabrication and unveiling. The town kept it. What else do you do with a fifteen-metre drop of water.
  3. The Half: one half of something, cleanly flanged down its open face, waiting for a second half the foundry never sent. Bolt-holes ready. Fifty years of birds using the open side as a grandstand.
  4. The disputed animal: a concrete beast repainted so many times its species is now a town argument — ram, working dog, something older. The servo sells postcards that hedge.
  5. The Gnomon: a sundial fin the size of a tennis court, casting accurate time onto bare paddock — the dial plate with its numbers was stage two, and there was no stage two.
  6. Open from the highway: the twenty-minute approach, the shape on the shimmer, Katita driving, Leif watching it refuse to become anything — and the pull-over decision made by neither of them aloud.
  7. The bird beat: they arrive at dusk and the thing is full — starlings pouring into an aperture like water finding a drain, the steel audibly alive. They eat the cooled pie on the one good plank and watch it fill.
  8. Katita's single photograph, and her refusal to take a second when the light improves. One record. Leif, who would archive it from nine angles, says nothing and files the fact of her one photograph somewhere deeper than the camera could reach. Render as behaviour, not cogitation.
  9. The plaque read aloud in full, including the blank: she reads the raised edges and then the worn middle — "erected by public subscription … to mark … " — and lets the gap stand as spoken silence. Nobody fills it.
  10. End driving away: the object in the mirrors for another twenty minutes, shrinking back into shimmer, the radio finding a station, the day going on being a good day. The last look back is Leif's, and the prose does not say so twice.

Exit condition

One page. They stop, they stand in it, they leave — and the reader laughs somewhere in the first third and can't fully account for their mood by the last line. Nothing is explained, nothing is mourned aloud, nothing connects to anything the characters are building. If the town's attempt to be seen from the highway registers as feeling rather than as observation, the fleck has done its work. Flash and vanish; the birds keep the building.
