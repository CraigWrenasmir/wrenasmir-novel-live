SCENE SCAFFOLD

The Tapping Returns

Rank: Ten Years of Aftermath Tier: Fleck · 1 p Plan §21 Band: train Law: PLANT — RESOLUTION BELONGS TO THE ARRIVAL

The job — what this scene must accomplish

A fleck: a hand doing something the man hasn't asked it to do. Three loads:

  • Return the tapping as bodily memory. Leif taps a rhythm unconsciously — on a motel table, on the tank of the motorbike, against the train window. Her protocol is still running in him, a decade after the last game.
  • Let it be five things at once, none of them named: Katita's prime-number game · the train ostinato from page one · bodily memory · a communication protocol · a musical phrase awaiting resolution. The writer holds all five; the page holds only a hand and a rhythm.
  • Plant, and refuse to pay off. The resolution — the slowing train aligning with the tapping and resolving it by one added beat — belongs to the Arrival (pp. 44–49). This fleck's entire discipline is ending on the unstressed beat.

Fuse ruling (Craig, 2026-07-22): the plant page (Primes on Skin) now closes the circuit openly — it ends with present-day Leif tapping fifty-seven into the train window. This page's job has therefore INVERTED from revelation to echo: the reader already knows the rhythm survives, so this fleck must pass almost unremarked — no re-explanation of the wire, no "he realised it was her game" (the reader was told; Leif never is), no callback furniture. The subtlety IS the content: her protocol running in him as unremarkably as breathing. The numeral and "prime" bans below now matter more, not less.

Flash and vanish: one paragraph-cluster, in and out before the reader can be sure what they heard.

Dialogue permissions

The prose may

Notate the rhythm physically — knuckle, fingerpad, glass, formica, gloved leather — and temporally: gap, gap, longer gap. Let a reader who wants to count, count; give them nothing that counts for them. Move the rhythm across surfaces and let each surface change its voice while the phrase stays the same.

Leif may

Notice the sound before he notices the hand — look for its source and find himself. Stop the hand with the other hand. Have it resume a sentence later. Mistake the rhythm for the start of a song he can't place, and fail to find the melody that would finish it.

No one may

Say prime. Put numbers on the page — no 2, 3, 5, 7, no counting in the narration. Attribute the rhythm to Katita by name or by unambiguous memory. Hear the train in it aloud. Resolve the phrase, complete it, or let any external rhythm lock with it. Call it a habit, a symptom, or a message — no motif knows it's a motif.

Sensory bank — raw material, take what serves

  • Motel staging: a formica table with a cigarette burn like a full stop; the glass ashtray that rings faintly on the third tap and not the others; the wall-mounted heater's off-cycle click keeping different time.
  • Motorbike staging: waiting at a level crossing, engine idling, gloved fingers on the tank — the crossing bells at their fixed tempo, his fingers at theirs, the two phasing (the bells are Arrival-playlist material; one touch here, no comment).
  • Train staging: the window cold against the knuckle; the bogies' rhythm arriving through the glass; fingerpad and fingernail giving two different notes on the same pane.
  • The hand itself: older than he thinks of it as being; the small muscles moving with the economy of long practice at something he doesn't remember practising.
  • The gap in the phrase — the rest, the wait — as the part his finger is most insistent about.
  • Ambient timekeepers to phase against (choose one): dripping tap, indicator stalk, keno draw chime through a wall, windscreen wipers of a parked car, the heater's thermostat.

Traps — the known failure modes

  • Cashing the resolution early. The single worst failure available in this rank: any alignment that "clicks" — with bells, bogies, wipers, anything — spends the Arrival's one irreplaceable payoff. Here, rhythms may approach, phase, and miss. Always miss.
  • Arithmetic on the page. The moment a number appears, the motif becomes a puzzle and the reader starts solving instead of hearing. No counts, no sequences, no "he tapped five times."
  • Attribution. "Katita's game" in any wording is the motif reading its own name tag. The memory of the forearm in the dark may shadow the passage; it may not enter it.
  • The five identities listed. Prime game, ostinato, memory, protocol, phrase — that inventory is scaffold material. If the prose enumerates what the tapping "is," cut the paragraph.
  • Overstaying. This is a fleck. At a page it is already at maximum length; at a page and a half it has become a scene and the vanish is lost.
  • Meaningful witnesses. No stranger may hear the rhythm and react significantly. (A wrong, friendly, oblivious tap-back is permitted — see spark 6 — but nobody understands anything.)

Ten sparks — optional ways in, steal freely

  1. Open on the sound, not the hand: a small knocking somewhere in the room, and the paragraph looking for its source before finding it at the end of his own arm.
  2. The level crossing: bells at their fixed tempo, fingers at theirs, the two beating against each other toward a meeting point — and the boom gates lift and he rides on before the rhythms can agree.
  3. Against the train window: the bogies propose a count through the glass and his fingers answer with a different one — call and response that keeps politely declining to become a duet.
  4. He stops the hand by holding it with the other hand, the way you'd quiet a small animal — and the sentence moves on without looking back. Two paragraphs later, unremarked, it is going again.
  5. The same phrase on three surfaces across the fleck — formica, glass, denim — each surface changing the timbre, the phrase itself never varying. An instrument being carried from room to room.
  6. A stranger across the aisle taps back — cheerful, wrong, meaningless — and the reply lands as the loneliest sound in the rank: the protocol reaching a node that doesn't run it. Wordless; no one understands anything.
  7. He decides it is the start of a song he can't place and spends a sentence reaching for the melody that would resolve it. There isn't one. He files it under songs, which is wrong, which is fine.
  8. Make the rest the protagonist: the gap in the phrase, the wait his finger insists on — the space where something is owed. Let the reader lean into that silence without being told it's a debt.
  9. He wakes with the hand already going — it kept the night shift — and the day's first feeling is of having interrupted something that was managing perfectly well without him.
  10. End mid-phrase, on the unstressed beat: the paragraph stops where the rhythm doesn't, and whatever sentence follows the fleck begins very slightly off the count.

Exit condition

Under a page — one paragraph-cluster, flash and vanish. It ends unresolved by construction: the phrase still open, no rhythm locked, no name attached, the hand either stilled mid-beat or left going as the prose looks away. The test: a reader who taps the passage out on their own table should feel a beat missing and not know they've been taught to wait for it. If anything in the fleck clicks, aligns, or completes, the Arrival has been robbed — cut until it misses.
