SCENE SCAFFOLD

Play Before Mission

Rank: Origins & Pleasure Tier: Medium · 2 pp Band: Vaporwave Plan §7: shared digital worlds ROOT born from play, not fear

The job — what this scene must accomplish

This block is the ratified correction to ROOT's origin story: before it is a mission it is a shared appetite. Two pages of a couple at play in digital worlds, ending with the first module built as a toy. Three loads:

  • Establish the shared worlds as pleasure. Obsolete games, a SecondLife-era Newcastle, hacker magazines read for fun, abandoned servers visited like beaches. The pixel-pastoral law: the hardware is tiny and the light is enormous.
  • Give the vaporwave register its human origin. Decades later ROOT's diagnostic space will speak to Leif in low-poly harbour light. That render only devastates if the reader met this aesthetic first as the couple's private dialect of joy.
  • The first module arrives as a joke or a gift — a dare made physical, a box that does one useless lovely thing. No mission statement, no collapse-talk, no founding. ROOT must be traceable back to an ice-cream container, not a manifesto.

Ratified guardrail: ROOT should arise from pleasure, play and curiosity, not simply fear of collapse. If a paragraph must be cut, cut anything that smells of purpose.

Where it sits

Currently remainder pp. 4–5 (≈ MS pp. 56–57) — after Primes on Skin, before River Bathing. The order can shift on the Pressure Map, but the logic holds anywhere in the Origins band: the private bodily language extends onto screens, then into hardware, then (two pages later) out onto the road.

Feeds directly into: the road years — the truck is this appetite with wheels — and, in the Road rank, the Heritage Tour of the Dead Server (a separate block: the tour with the three silent avatars is staged THERE, not here; this page may only plant the habit). Pays off from: the rooftop's hacker-magazine / BBS lore mention and the napkin diagram — the graph problem now has a playground.

Dialogue permissions

Katita may

Narrate what the renderer is doing wrong with affection. Keep dead servers' addresses the way other people keep phone numbers. Treat a game's physics as a maths problem she refuses to solve because solving it would end the game. Issue the dare that becomes the first module. Name the module something stupid.

Leif may

Give a guided tour of a place that no longer exists — showing off, not curating. Read the classified ads in a photocopied hacker zine aloud in bed. Prefer the misrendered harbour at one specific in-game hour and arrange their sessions around it. Solder badly. Keep screenshots in a folder he treats like a photo album.

No one may

Say collapse, preserve, archive, the future, backup — or pitch anything. Call the aesthetic by any name (the word "vaporwave" does not exist for them). Observe that the light is beautiful because the hardware is small — that's the scaffold's thesis, not theirs. Declare the module "a start" or "version one." The first module is for nothing, and both of them must be allowed to leave it at that.

Sensory bank — raw material, take what serves

  • A dark flat lit by one CRT and one flat-screen of different colour temperatures; the tower's fan; router lights doing their small civic duty in the corner.
  • Two chairs jammed at one desk; heat off the tower against their shins; one keyboard passed back and forth — or split: WASD his, mouse hers, one body operated between them.
  • The low-poly harbour: water a single repeating tile; the horizon with a visible seam; coal ships as brick-shaped suggestions; draw-distance fog eating Stockton; buildings the real city has since taken down still standing in the render, unremarked.
  • The abandoned server at 2 a.m.: her avatar's default walk (no one bought animations here); a sun jammed at permanent late afternoon by whichever admin last touched the sky and never came back.
  • Sounds: MIDI loops from an old game left running for the music; a winamp playlist off the second machine; rain on the real window while the render offers no weather at all.
  • Hacker magazines: photocopied, mail-ordered, already old when they get them; staple rust; the classifieds better than the exploits.
  • The workbench creep: soldering smell in the kitchen, a $4 op-shop router flashed after midnight, breadboard and stripped wire in a takeaway container, then the ice-cream container that becomes the first casing.
  • Pixel-pastoral light: an enormous square sun, grass in two greens, shadows that move in whole steps — light falling in integers.
  • The real dawn arriving mid-session, embarrassing both screens.

Traps — the known failure modes

  • The retro listicle. If the block becomes an inventory of period technology, it's furniture, not scene. Keep bodies in the room: two chairs, one keyboard, shins against the hot tower, the dawn.
  • Mission creep. One sentence of purpose kills the block. The module is a dare made physical; nobody may know what it's for, because it isn't for anything.
  • Spending the heritage tour. Visiting the dead server here is allowed; the guided tour with followers is a separate, later block. Don't burn the three avatars.
  • Omen-lighting the render. No character may find the low-poly world "like memory" or dwell on its melancholy aloud. Its future weight must be invisible.
  • Era soup. SecondLife-era is roughly mid-2000s: ADSL, CRTs on the way out, no smartphones yet. The hacker mags are already relics when they read them — that's the point. Keep the modem handshake out entirely; war-dialling owns that sound.
  • Summary-time drift. "They spent whole winters in there" is the disease. Two sessions at most, minutes inside each; texture the rest by implication.

Ten sparks — optional ways in, steal freely

  1. Open inside the render, not the room: two avatars standing on one-polygon water, and only after a paragraph the fan, the shins, the rain on the real window — the book's ontological wobble delivered as pure texture, no comment.
  2. She swims her avatar straight out past the draw distance to see what the world does when it runs out. It keeps laying one more tile of water just ahead of her, forever. She is delighted for days and never says why.
  3. The dead server's sky: a sun somebody set to late afternoon years ago and never came back for. Leif brings her there the way you'd bring someone to a beach at the right hour. His first tour.
  4. The dare: she bets him the router can't be made to say anything. A week later a $4 box in an ice-cream container plays one bird call to the backyard at dusk. They drink to it and name it something stupid.
  5. One keyboard, two operators: keys his, mouse hers, an argument conducted entirely in movement — the avatar walking somewhere neither of them chose alone.
  6. The zine classifieds read aloud like race results: mail-order antennas, a man in Lismore selling "slightly haunted" hard drives, a PO box in Wauchope that answers in green biro.
  7. In the render the coal ships never move. She counts them anyway — four, same as last week — one touch of the rooftop's counting, no comment.
  8. Mid-harbour they find another player's abandoned house, furniture arranged for a life, a chair facing a window. They don't touch anything. Both are quiet in the real room afterwards, and neither says why.
  9. He screenshots; she never does. One line each, buried in different paragraphs, no adjacency, no gloss.
  10. End in the real backyard: the module's single LED visible from the kitchen window, pulsing at whatever rate it pulses, the two of them watching it the way you watch a fire — enormous dark, tiny hardware, the law reversed into the real world.

Exit condition

Two pages. It ends with the toy alive in the backyard and no plans made — no next step, no "what if we," no gaze toward the horizon. The reader should think: a young couple mucking about with old games and junk electronics, sweet and slightly nerdy. Nothing may announce that the church they will spend their lives building is already standing in the yard in an ice-cream container. If the block ends feeling like an origin story, it has failed; it should end feeling like a Tuesday.
